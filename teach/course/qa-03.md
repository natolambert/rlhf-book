## "RL is basically just SFT with a regularization term?"

A great question from the Lecture 3 comments (condensed):

> The conventional belief is that RL is good for hardcore reasoning whereas SFT is good for structure and formatting — which is basically why we've had 2-3 phases in post-training for so long. But papers keep popping up saying RL is basically just SFT with some regularization term, and if you do their regularized SFT you get the same result — with plots to prove it. **How believable / generalizable is this? I can't imagine it generalizes to big models; otherwise DeepSeek and GLM would already be doing it.**

<!-- step -->

Short version: **the math is real, the plots are probably real, and the generalization claim is doing all of the heavy lifting.**

Let's take it in three steps: why the equivalence keeps getting rediscovered, what it hides, and how to read these papers.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: viewer question on [Lecture 3](https://rlhfbook.com/course) -->

---

## Why these papers keep appearing: the math really is close

The policy gradient is a **weighted maximum-likelihood update on the model's own samples**:

$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\!\left[ A(x, y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \right] $$

Squint, and SFT is the special case where the "advantage" is 1 on curated samples and 0 everywhere else:

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} = -\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^\star \mid x) $$

<!-- step -->

This isn't a new observation — the field has been *using* it for years:

- **Rejection sampling / RAFT** [@dong2023raft]: generate, filter by reward, SFT on the winners. Literally "RL as SFT," and it's a real pipeline stage (Llama 2 [@touvron2023llama], DeepSeek R1 [@guo2025deepseek]).
- The new crop of papers dresses the weights up differently (importance ratios, KL-derived weights, baselines folded into the data filter) and shows a regularized SFT loss matching GRPO-style RL on their benchmarks.

## What the one-step equivalence hides

The equivalences are typically **per-update**, from a fixed sampling distribution. Training is not one update.

<!-- step -->

- **On-policy freshness.** RL re-draws samples from the *current* policy every batch. The "equivalent" SFT dataset is equivalent for one step, then goes stale. Keep regenerating it and you've quietly rebuilt online RL — with the same cost and infrastructure.
- **Negative gradients.** GRPO/PPO push probability *down* on below-average samples. Vanilla SFT has no push-down term; the regularizers are there to imitate it, and imitations leak.
- **Exploration & credit assignment.** Long reasoning chains reward the model for finding *new* successful trajectories, not re-weighting old ones.
- **The evidence regime.** These results are mostly ≤8B models, math-style verifiable benchmarks, short runs — exactly the regime where SFT/distillation from a stronger teacher is already known to be strong (see Lecture 7).

<!-- footnote-right: Related: on-policy distillation and its limits, [Lecture 7](https://rlhfbook.com/teach/course/lec7-chap12-synthetic-data/) -->

---

## How believable? My take

- **Believable:** the gradient algebra, and the small-scale results on the reported benchmarks.
- **Unproven:** that any of it holds at frontier scale, on broad capability suites, over long training runs.

<!-- step -->

Your sociological argument is basically right — and it cuts deeper than it looks:

- Labs would **love** an SFT-shaped replacement for RL: cheaper, stabler, trivially parallel. The big RL stacks (R1, o-series, GLM) were built *after* the cheap alternatives were tried.
- Frontier pipelines **already use** the SFT-shaped versions where they win: rejection sampling and distillation are core stages, not curiosities.
- The 2-3 phase structure isn't the field failing to notice the gradients look similar. Phases persist because the **data** is different — demonstrations vs. verifiable rewards vs. preferences — not just the loss.

<!-- step -->

**Pocket rule:** treat "RL = regularized SFT" papers as useful theory plus small-scale evidence. The claim to watch for is a held-out result at large scale on general capabilities — nobody has shown one yet.

## "How does the ~1M SFT prompt budget scale with model size?"

From the Lecture 2 comments:

> You mention some pocket rules for prompt budgets, e.g. ~1M prompts for SFT. In your experience, how does this number scale with the size of the model being post-trained?

<!-- step -->

Honestly — **it doesn't change a ton.**

- The ~1M figure (Tülu 3 scale [@lambert2024t]) is calibrated for roughly **4B-parameter models and up** — around the size where models really start to learn from post-training.
- Larger models need a bit **less**, not more: the easy data stops teaching them anything, so the effective budget shrinks from the easy end.

<!-- step -->

What actually moves with scale is the **mixture**, not the count: you trim the easy tail and re-weight toward the hard, reasoning-heavy prompts that still have gradient signal.

## Correction: rejection sampling selects an *index*, not a completion (Lecture 1)

A sharp-eyed viewer flagged the rejection-sampling loss on the Lecture 1 deck (slide 41):

> (Typo?) Should $y_i^\star = \arg\max_j \ldots$ be $y^\star = y_{\arg\max_j \ldots}$?

What the slide showed:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RS}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}\!\left(\theta;\; \{(x_i, y_i^{\star})\}\right) \quad \text{where } y_i^{\star} \;{\color{red}\boxed{\color{red}=}}\; \arg\max_{j} r_\phi(x_i, y_{i,j}) $$

<!-- step -->

Correct: $\arg\max_j$ returns the winning **index** $j$, not the completion itself. The fix selects the completion *at* that index (the subscript on $y^\star$ stays $i$ — one winner per prompt):

$$ y_i^{\star} = y_{i,\, \arg\max_{j} r_\phi(x_i, y_{i,j})} $$

Fixed on the live deck: [Lecture 1](https://rlhfbook.com/teach/course/lec1-chap1-3/) · [PR #481](https://github.com/natolambert/rlhf-book/pull/481).
