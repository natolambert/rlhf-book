## "Should capabilities be trained separately, then merged?"

A viewer asks:

> Hi Nathan, thanks for another great video on post-training! At @24:21, this got me thinking (and I might be misunderstanding): if each capability is sort of its own "mode," would it make sense to post-train stage by stage — coding → math → agentic tool use → etc. — learning one mode at a time? My guess is this intuition is wrong somewhere, since it seems like people don't really do this and still put a lot of care into the data mixture. Would love to understand what I'm missing here.

<!-- step -->

**Increasingly: yes as an engineering decomposition — not because the capabilities are actually independent.**

- The old version — sequentially finetune one checkpoint on math, then code, then tools — is brittle. Later stages can overwrite earlier capabilities.
- The emerging version trains **specialist teachers in parallel**, then consolidates them into one student with multi-teacher on-policy distillation (MOPD).
- "Merge later" means **distill behavior in policy space**, not average checkpoints.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: [@mcmc6052 on YouTube (Lecture 10)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwpYxANrpUs&lc=UgwCZ6w8z4aPayBK3pB4AaABAg) -->

---

<!-- columns: 62/38 -->
## Separate the climbs; integrate the behaviors

![MiMo-V2-Flash trains domain specialists before consolidating them into one student with MOPD.](assets/mimo-v2-flash-recipe.png)

|||

- Each specialist gets its own environment and reward.
- The student samples; the matching teacher supplies dense token-level feedback.
- In controlled comparisons, MOPD beat mixed RL, cascade RL, off-policy finetuning, and parameter merging.

<!-- footnote-right: Sources: [MiMo-V2-Flash](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02780), [MOPD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30406) -->

---

## The student must reach the teacher's neighborhood

MOPD scores **student-generated trajectories**. If the student never visits a reasoning path or tool-use pattern, the teacher cannot directly supervise it there.

Nemotron 3 Ultra found this support mismatch when some specialist teachers had substantially different SFT histories: straightforward MOPD recovered agentic skills better than unseen reasoning capabilities.

<!-- step -->

The practical recipe:

**shared SFT seed** $\rightarrow$ **specialist climbs** $\rightarrow$ **bridge with demonstrations or fresh RL if needed** $\rightarrow$ **MOPD** $\rightarrow$ **joint evaluation**

**Pocket rule:** stage by domain when environments and rewards genuinely differ — not by default. MOPD is a way to consolidate specialists, not evidence that capabilities are independent.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: [Nemotron 3 Ultra, §3.3](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nemotron/files/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Ultra-Technical-Report.pdf) -->

## "Why does RLVR often omit the KL penalty?"

A follow-up from the Lecture 10 comments (condensed):

> Is KL removed because verifiable rewards do not create the same proxy-reward overoptimization problem as learned reward models?

<!-- step -->

**Often, yes — for the cleanest version of RLVR.** The KL penalty buys conservatism:

$$
J(\pi)
=
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}[r(x,y)]
-
\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}).
$$

It protects against exploiting an imperfect reward and destroying behavior the reference model already had. If neither is the bottleneck, KL is mostly a tax on learning.

But **verifiable means checked, not necessarily correctly specified.**

<!-- footnote-right: Source: [@mcmc6052 follow-up on YouTube (Lecture 10)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IwpYxANrpUs&lc=UgwCZ6w8z4aPayBK3pB4AaABAg.AZq-x8qDJuuAZq0_-kE-33) -->

---

<!-- columns: 50/50 -->
## When $\beta=0$ is the better tradeoff

### Why clean RLVR drops KL

- A correct answer or passing test is closer to the real objective than a learned preference score.
- A fixed-reference KL penalizes **all** novelty, including genuinely better reasoning.
- Dropping the reference-model forward pass saves memory and compute.

Open-Reasoner-Zero, Magistral, and Skywork OR-1 all used versions of this recipe [@hu2025openreasonerzero; @mistral2025magistral; @he2025skyworkor1].

|||

### Bring regularization back when

- the verifier is a learned judge, weak test suite, or other incomplete proxy;
- preserving general capabilities, safety behavior, style, or tool protocol matters;
- $\beta=0$ causes measured regressions, exploitability, or collapse.

For stale or off-policy rollouts, the needed control may instead be relative to the **behavior policy**, not the initial model.

**Pocket rule:** start at $\beta=0$ only when the verifier is trustworthy and held-out evaluations say the drift is harmless.

## "How does the ~1M SFT prompt budget scale with model size?"

From the Lecture 2 comments:

> You mention some pocket rules for prompt budgets, e.g. ~1M prompts for SFT. In your experience, how does this number scale with the size of the model being post-trained?

<!-- step -->

Honestly — **it doesn't change a ton.**

- The ~1M figure (Tülu 3 scale [@lambert2024t]) is calibrated for roughly **4B-parameter models and up** — around the size where models really start to learn from post-training.
- Larger models need a bit **less**, not more: the easy data stops teaching them anything, so the effective budget shrinks from the easy end.

<!-- step -->

What actually moves with scale is the **mixture**, not the count: you trim the easy tail and re-weight toward the hard, reasoning-heavy prompts that still have gradient signal.

[Tülu 3 405B](https://allenai.org/blog/tulu-3-405B) saw the same pattern on the RL side: at 405B, specialized MATH-only data beat the mixed GSM8K + IFEval recipe used for smaller models — bigger models want the harder, specialized end of the mix.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: [@secmt on YouTube (Lecture 2)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4gIwiSPmQkU&lc=Ugy_HdDeTwJzC87rsOZ4AaABAg); budgets from [@lambert2024t] -->

## "RL is basically just SFT with a regularization term?"

A great question from the Lecture 3 comments (condensed):

> The conventional belief is that RL is good for hardcore reasoning whereas SFT is good for structure and formatting — which is basically why we've had 2-3 phases in post-training for so long. But papers keep popping up saying RL is basically just SFT with some regularization term, and if you do their regularized SFT you get the same result — with plots to prove it. **How believable / generalizable is this? I can't imagine it generalizes to big models; otherwise DeepSeek and GLM would already be doing it.**

<!-- step -->

On the premise: I'd say it's less "SFT for formatting, RL for reasoning" and more that **SFT is the foundation** (and very sample-efficient), then **RL is how the model learns a lot more from there** — on harder problems, and adapting to the harnesses it'll be used in at deployment.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: [@entropic4439 on YouTube (Lecture 3)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K_Sj_-1BUMM&lc=UgzCuTOWvBCSKV5lPGB4AaABAg) -->

---

## The gradients look alike — the training loop doesn't

The policy gradient and the SFT gradient share the same log-prob term:

$$ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)}\!\left[ A(x, y)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) \right] $$

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} = -\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y^\star \mid x) $$

On curated targets, SFT is the $A = 1$ special case. This shared algebra drives iw-SFT [@qin2025supervised], DFT [@wu2025generalization], and the unified-view papers [@lv2025unified].

<!-- step -->

What the algebra leaves out:

- **Negative advantages.** RL pushes below-baseline samples *down*; SFT only pushes curated targets up.
- **On-policy data.** RL resamples every batch. A fixed SFT dataset grows stale — iw-SFT's own bound loosens as the model moves away from it.
- **Scale.** The evidence is from small math-specialized models. DeepSeek-V3.2 puts over 10% of pre-training compute into RL [@deepseek2025v32].

<!-- footnote-right: Sources: [@qin2025supervised], [@wu2025generalization], [@lv2025unified], [@deepseek2025v32] -->
