## "RL is basically just SFT with a regularization term?"

A great question from the Lecture 3 comments (condensed):

> The conventional belief is that RL is good for hardcore reasoning whereas SFT is good for structure and formatting — which is basically why we've had 2-3 phases in post-training for so long. But papers keep popping up saying RL is basically just SFT with some regularization term, and if you do their regularized SFT you get the same result — with plots to prove it. **How believable / generalizable is this? I can't imagine it generalizes to big models; otherwise DeepSeek and GLM would already be doing it.**

<!-- step -->

First, the premise needs an upgrade — the recipe isn't "SFT for formatting, RL for reasoning":

- **SFT is the foundation, and it's sample-efficient** — it moves the model the most per example and sets up everything after it.
- **RL is how the model learns a lot more from there** — especially on harder problems — and how it **adapts to the harnesses it will be deployed in**.

The phases persist because they do different jobs on different data, not because nobody noticed the losses look similar.

<!-- footnote-right: Source: viewer question on [Lecture 3](https://rlhfbook.com/course) -->

---

## The literature: the papers are real — read the fine print

The 2025-26 wave, one line each:

- **iw-SFT** [@qin2025supervised]: SFT on curated data optimizes a *lower bound* on the RL objective; importance weights tighten it. Qwen2.5-32B, 1k curated traces.
- **DFT** [@wu2025generalization]: the SFT gradient is secretly a policy gradient with a hidden $1/\pi_\theta$ reward; a one-line reweight "fixes" it. ≤8B math-specialized models.
- **Proximal SFT** [@zhu2025proximal]: SFT = policy gradient with advantage $\equiv 1$, so borrow PPO's clipping. A stability claim, not a replacement claim.
- **Unified view / HPT** [@lv2025unified]: SFT and RL gradients are one estimator under *different data-distribution assumptions*.

<!-- step -->

Read closely and **none of them claims equivalence**:

- They prove **lower bounds and gradient identities** — on small math-specialized models, against author-run RL baselines.
- The unified-view paper's own algorithm still **needs live RL rollouts** (it adaptively mixes SFT and RL, it doesn't replace RL).
- The lineage is old anyway: rejection sampling / RAFT [@dong2023raft] has been "SFT as crude RL" in production recipes for years.

<!-- footnote-right: Sources: [@qin2025supervised], [@wu2025generalization], [@zhu2025proximal], [@lv2025unified] -->

---

## The intuition: the identities are anchored, RL is not

Each of these results holds **relative to a fixed data distribution**. That's the tell.

- iw-SFT says it plainly: its bound on the RL objective **loosens as the policy drifts** away from the data-generating policy during training.
- On-policy RL never has this problem: it **re-samples from the current model every batch**, so as the model improves, its training data improves with it. That loop is what you're paying for on hard problems — and it's where the model learns to operate the agentic harnesses it ships inside.

<!-- step -->

And the field is voting with its compute:

- DeepSeek-V3.2 allocates **over 10% of pre-training compute to RL** [@deepseek2025v32]; every frontier open recipe (R1, Qwen3, Kimi K2, GLM) ships large RL stages.
- Where SFT-shaped training *does* win — smaller models distilled from a stronger teacher — labs already use it. That is exactly the regime these papers test.

**Pocket rule:** the gradient algebra is real, the small-scale plots are probably real, and the generalization claim is unproven where it matters — your instinct is right. (The generalization *and forgetting* comparisons of RL vs. SFT are a separate, additional argument — coming in Lectures 9/10.)

<!-- footnote-right: Sources: [@qin2025supervised], [@deepseek2025v32] -->

## "How does the ~1M SFT prompt budget scale with model size?"

From the Lecture 2 comments:

> You mention some pocket rules for prompt budgets, e.g. ~1M prompts for SFT. In your experience, how does this number scale with the size of the model being post-trained?

<!-- step -->

Honestly — **it doesn't change a ton.**

- The ~1M figure (Tülu 3 scale [@lambert2024t]) is calibrated for roughly **4B-parameter models and up** — around the size where models really start to learn from post-training.
- Larger models need a bit **less**, not more: the easy data stops teaching them anything, so the effective budget shrinks from the easy end.

<!-- step -->

What actually moves with scale is the **mixture**, not the count: you trim the easy tail and re-weight toward the hard, reasoning-heavy prompts that still have gradient signal.

## Correction: rejection sampling selects an *index*, not a completion (Lecture 1)

A sharp-eyed viewer flagged the rejection-sampling loss on the Lecture 1 deck (slide 41):

> (Typo?) Should $y_i^\star = \arg\max_j \ldots$ be $y^\star = y_{\arg\max_j \ldots}$?

What the slide showed:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RS}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}\!\left(\theta;\; \{(x_i, y_i^{\star})\}\right) \quad \text{where } y_i^{\star} \;{\color{red}\boxed{\color{red}=}}\; \arg\max_{j} r_\phi(x_i, y_{i,j}) $$

<!-- step -->

Correct: $\arg\max_j$ returns the winning **index** $j$, not the completion itself. The fix selects the completion *at* that index (the subscript on $y^\star$ stays $i$ — one winner per prompt):

$$ y_i^{\star} = y_{i,\, \arg\max_{j} r_\phi(x_i, y_{i,j})} $$

Fixed on the live deck: [Lecture 1](https://rlhfbook.com/teach/course/lec1-chap1-3/) · [PR #481](https://github.com/natolambert/rlhf-book/pull/481).
